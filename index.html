01 · Thinking Principles

Three Notes on Modeling Reality Honestly

Click any note to expand. No buzzwords — just the working rules that guide how I write code and test hypotheses.

Kierkegaard noticed something every quantitative researcher eventually learns the hard way: looking backward makes chaos look neat and predictable. In a Jupyter notebook, you already have the entire historical CSV sitting in memory. If a rolling average, scaler, or target label accidentally peeks even one day ahead (t + 1), your backtest looks effortless.

Real markets don't hand you tomorrow's row. Treating strict timestamp cutoffs as a non-negotiable rule — where the model at time t is completely blind to anything after t — is the difference between honest research and fooling yourself.

The central idea in Marcus Aurelius's Meditations is separating what belongs to your judgment from external events you don't control. That maps cleanly onto engineering. You cannot stop volatility from spiking, correlations from breaking, or an external LLM API from throwing HTTP 429 rate-limit errors under load.

What you can control is how your system behaves when calm weather ends: modeling jump risk explicitly (Bates SVJD rather than smooth Gaussian assumptions alone), outputting calibrated uncertainty intervals instead of naked point guesses, and wiring fallback circuit breakers before shipping to users.

In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Kahneman shows how easily humans mistake a fluent, confident story for a true one. Large language models have the exact same flaw: ask an ungrounded model for a revenue number from an SEC 10-K filing and it will state a fabricated figure in polished prose (scoring 1.67 / 5.0 on faithfulness in my baseline tests).

Whether in financial Q&A, competition post-processing, or option calibration, fluency means nothing without verification — grounding answers in retrieved primary filings, checking analytical sensitivities, and testing code on a clean machine from scratch.
